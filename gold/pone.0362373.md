# Gold answers: Beyond generalizations: Understanding adolescent social networks in American Indian communities

Paper `pone.0362373`, 19 pages. Status: reviewed by the labeler 2026-11-22.

Origin codes: R1 to R4 are reviewers with the review round in parentheses, AR1 and AR2 are the author responses, ED is the editor, and `paper` means the answer comes from the published paper alone.

## Question

Acceptable:

- **Q1** How demographic attributes, substance use, depression and American Indian identity are associated with friendship formation in AI-majority reservation schools. (R1(1), R2(2), R3(2))
  - p. 2: "we examine how demographic attributes, substance use behaviors, depression, and AI identity are associated with friendship formation within these AI-majority educational contexts"
- **Q2** Whether patterns found in racially heterogeneous schools, where substance use is linked to friendship, hold where the student body is predominantly American Indian. (R1(1), R2(2), R3(2))
  - p. 2: "It remains unclear whether these patterns hold in settings where the student population is predominantly AI"
- **Q3** The role of substance use in the formation of friendship ties in these schools. (R2(2), AR2)
  - p. 2: "investigating the role of substance use in the formation of friendship ties within"
- **Q4** What structural patterns characterize these friendship networks, in particular reciprocity and ties among friends of friends? (paper)
  - p. 1: "This study examines the structural and relational foundations of friendship networks among AI adolescents"
  - p. 14: "Our analysis focused on endogenous structural factors"

Rejected:

- **Q-x1** How peer networks influence substance use among American Indian adolescents. (R2(2) on the revised abstract, AR2)
  - Reviewer 2 warned that the abstract read this way. The study is about friendship formation, and the authors reworded the abstract.
  - p. 17: "nor as evidence that friendship networks causally shape these attributes"
- **Q-x2** Whether friendship processes differ between American Indian and non-AI youth, tested by comparing the two. (R1(1) on the submitted draft, R2(2), AR1)
  - The draft claimed a comparison. Every participant is from an AI-majority school; the published paper offers only indirect comparison with earlier studies.
  - p. 6: "it provides a basis for indirect comparison with patterns reported in prior non-AI studies"

## Central claim

Acceptable:

- **C1** In these schools friendship is organized by structural mechanisms (reciprocity, transitivity) and demographic similarity; substance use was not a consistent basis for friendship ties. (R1(1), R3(2))
  - p. 1: "peer ties are organized around structural and demographic commonalities rather than risky behaviors"
  - p. 1: "substance use showed no consistent association with sending ties or receiving ties"
- **C2** Substance use is a weaker organizing principle for friendship here than in the racially heterogeneous settings that dominate earlier research. (R1(1), R3(2), AR2)
  - p. 14: "substance use operates as a weaker organizing principle for friendship formation in AI reservation schools than in the racially heterogeneous settings"
- **C3** The findings challenge an assumption behind many network-based prevention programs and call for culturally grounded theory and interventions. (R3(2))
  - p. 1: "These findings challenge assumptions underpinning many network-based prevention programs"

Rejected:

- **C-x1** The study shows, by comparison, that American Indian youth form friendships through different mechanisms than non-AI youth. (R1(1), R3(2), AR1)
  - Reviewer 1 called this the main mismatch between claim and analysis. There is no comparison sample, and the structural effects resemble those found elsewhere.
  - p. 16: "future research is needed to assess whether these patterns differ across settings"

## Evidence

Acceptable:

- **E1** A cross-sectional social network survey of 9th and 10th grade students in three schools on a Northern Plains reservation. (R1(1), R3(2))
  - p. 6: "to conduct a comprehensive cross-sectional social network survey among 9th- and 10th-grade students"
- **E2** 263 students completed the survey (a 77% response rate); the analysis uses 247 with complete network data. (R1(1))
  - p. 6: "for a total sample size of 263"
  - p. 6: "Of the 247 participants with complete network data"
- **E3** Exponential random graph models estimated separately for each school, with structural terms and actor attributes, reported as average marginal effects. (R1(1), R2(2), R3(2), AR1)
  - p. 8: "We employed Exponential Random Graph Models (ERGMs) to model the probability of friendship tie formation within each school"
  - p. 9: "we computed Average Marginal Effects (AMEs) using the ergMargins package"
- **E4** The networks come from directed friendship nominations: each student named up to 10 closest friends at the school. (R2(2))
  - p. 7: "select up to 10 friends you are closest with in grades 7th-12th at your school"
- **E5** Measures: self-reported use of alcohol, tobacco and marijuana as yes or no; a depression scale (CES-D); a six-item AI identity scale adapted from the MEIM. (R2(1), AR1)
  - p. 7: "was measured through binary variables"
  - p. 7: "was assessed using six items adapted from the Affirmation and Belonging subscale of the Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure"
- **E6** Missing substance use answers were filled in from peers' reports of the respondent's behavior, with a sensitivity analysis excluding those cases. (R2(1), R2(2), AR1, AR2)
  - p. 6: "we utilized available peer perception data to infer behavior"
- **E7** The three schools differ: a small tribal school in an isolated village, a larger tribal school, and a private college preparatory school. (R2(2), AR2)
  - p. 6: "a private, college preparatory school situated near the largest village in the area"

Rejected:

- **E-x1** The study includes a comparison sample of non-AI schools or students. (R1(1), R2(2), AR1)
  - Reviewer 1 asked for one; the authors explain why they could not provide it.
  - p. 16: "identifying a suitable dataset with comparable schools (similar size, rural location, and contemporary data) was not feasible"

## Key findings

Acceptable:

- **F1** [core] Substance use shows no consistent association with sending ties, receiving ties or homophily. The exceptions are isolated: an alcohol sender effect in School C (p < 0.05) and alcohol homophily in School B (p < 0.01), the largest school. (AR2, R1(1), R2(1), R2(2), R3(2))
  - p. 13: "Substance use demonstrated no consistent or robust association with friendship dynamics in this sample"
  - p. 13: "The only exception was a positive sender effect for alcohol use in School C"
  - p. 15: "Substance use homophily was detected only in School B"
  - p. 13: "Alcohol use showed significant homophily in School B (AME = 0.0039, p < 0.01)"
- **F2** [core] Reciprocity and transitivity are strong and consistent in all three schools. (R1(1), R3(2))
  - p. 13: "Reciprocity was positive and highly significant in every model"
- **F3** [core] Homophily on gender and grade is strong in all schools; homophily on sexual orientation is also positive. (R1(1), R3(2))
  - p. 13: "Strong and significant homophily was present for gender and grade across all schools"
- **F4** [secondary] Depression and AI identity are not substantively associated with friendship formation. (R2(1))
  - p. 13: "Depression sender and receiver effects were substantively negligible across all schools"
  - p. 15: "AI identity homophily was consistently non-significant across all schools"
- **F5** [secondary] Degree effects vary: outgoing ties are centralized in every school, while incoming ties are centralized only in School C, the private preparatory school. (R2(2), AR2)
  - p. 13: "a significant negative coefficient was observed only in School C"
- **F7** [secondary] The null substance use results hold in models with only structural terms and substance use, so they are not produced by the other covariates. (R1(1), R2(1), AR1)
  - p. 14: "This robustness check confirms that the null findings are not an artifact of model specification"
- **F8** [secondary] The structural effects match earlier research on non-AI adolescents in direction and relative size. (R1(1), R3(2), AR2)
  - p. 14: "Endogenous factors assessed in this study align with prior research in direction and relative magnitude"

Rejected:

- **F-x1** Reciprocity or transitivity is stronger among American Indian adolescents than in other populations. (R1(1), R3(2), AR2)
  - The paper expects this in its theory section. Reviewer 3 pointed out that the results cannot test it, and the discussion reports alignment with earlier work, not a stronger effect.
  - p. 14: "Endogenous factors assessed in this study align with prior research in direction and relative magnitude"
- **F-x2** Substance use, depression or AI identity has no causal effect on friendship formation. (R2(1), AR1)
  - The limitations restrict every result to an association.
  - p. 17: "not as evidence that substance use, depression, or AI identity causally affect friendship formation"
- **F-x3** The study found that American Indian cultural values (kinship, collectivism, egalitarian norms) shape how friendships form. (R1(1), R3(2))
  - Labeler, 2026-11-22: the cultural argument is theory (T2), not a finding. No cultural value is measured; the discussion offers it as something that 'may' explain a pattern.
  - p. 14: "may reflect the stronger operation of egalitarian norms in tribal school settings"

Neutral: no credit, no penalty:

- **F-n1** The authors characterize reciprocity, transitivity and demographic homophily as the primary drivers of friendship. (paper)
  - Labeler, 2026-11-22: the abstract's wording. Naming the three patterns earns F2 and F3; 'primary drivers' adds no finding. Quoted as the authors' characterization it is accurate: no credit, no penalty. Saying these factors caused friendships to form goes beyond the cross-sectional design.
  - p. 1: "were primary drivers of friendship"

## Scope and limits

Acceptable:

- **L1** Three schools on one reservation: the results may not extend to other reservations or to American Indian youth in racially heterogeneous schools. (R1(1), AR1)
  - p. 16: "the results presented here represent only three schools on one reservation"
- **L2** There is no comparison sample of non-AI schools; a suitable one could not be found. (R1(1), R2(2), R3(2), AR1)
  - p. 16: "identifying a suitable dataset with comparable schools (similar size, rural location, and contemporary data) was not feasible"
- **L3** The design is cross-sectional: it cannot separate selection from influence, and the results are associations, not causal effects. (R2(1), AR1)
  - p. 16: "cross-sectional ERGMs cannot analytically distinguish selection processes"
  - p. 17: "not as evidence that substance use, depression, or AI identity causally affect friendship formation"
- **L4** Developmental scope: the study covers ninth and tenth graders at one time point and cannot say whether the patterns hold at other ages or change during school transitions. (paper)
  - p. 16: "these data reflect only one developmental epoch of adolescents at one time point"
  - p. 16: "Relational patterns among AI youth will likely differ across the life course"
- **L5** Filling in missing substance use from peers' perceptions can be biased and could inflate apparent similarity among friends. (R2(2), AR2)
  - p. 7: "perceptions of friends’ substance use can be systematically biased"
- **L6** The demographic terms adjust for grade, gender and sexual orientation and describe friendship patterns along those dimensions. Their coefficients cannot be interpreted as independent causal effects. (R2(3))
  - p. 8: "we caution against interpreting the demographic coefficients as independent causal effects of demographics on tie formation"
  - p. 8: "We therefore report the demographic terms as descriptive patterns and robustness benchmarks"

Rejected:

- **L-x1** The authors state that with only three schools they cannot statistically disentangle the effects of specific school features. (AR2)
  - The authors say this in their response letter, twice. The sentence is not in the published paper, which presents the school-level explanations as things that 'may' hold. An author response is a candidate, like a review, and is checked against the paper.
- **L-x2** The authors note that earlier studies report no comparable effect sizes, which limits the comparison. (R3(2), AR2)
  - Reviewer 3 raised it and the authors answered at length in the response. The published paper explains why it reports average marginal effects but does not state this as a limitation.

## Theoretical positioning

Acceptable:

- **T1** [primary] [framework] The network analysis of friendship formation: endogenous structural processes (reciprocity, transitivity, degree effects) together with actor attributes acting through sender, receiver and homophily effects. (R1(1), R2(1), ED(1))
  - p. 3: "there are factors that derive from the network structure itself, known as endogenous factors"
- **T2** [secondary] [framework] A cultural-context argument: American Indian values of kinship, collectivism and egalitarianism shape how these network processes operate. (ED(1), R1(1), R2(1), AR1)
  - p. 3: "their prevalence and strength are not culturally neutral but are shaped by the social norms and relational expectations of a community"
- **T3** [stance] Tests whether findings from racially heterogeneous schools generalize to a setting where AI youth are the majority. It replicates the structural and demographic patterns and disputes the generalization of the link between substance use and friendship. (R2(2), R3(2))
  - p. 3: "this research moves beyond generalizations derived from prior research"
  - p. 14: "The absence of consistent substance use effects marks the most striking departure from the broader adolescent network literature"

Rejected:

- **T-x1** The paper is framed as a study of peer influence on substance use. (R2(2), AR2)
  - The abstract once read this way. The paper studies friendship selection and says it cannot separate selection from influence.
  - p. 16: "cross-sectional ERGMs cannot analytically distinguish selection processes"

## Interlocutor claim

Acceptable:

- **I1** Research in diverse schools: substance use is linked to friendship; users receive more nominations and friends share use. The paper disputes that this generalizes to AI-majority schools. (R1(1), R3(2), AR2)
  - p. 2: "Research in diverse school settings has consistently demonstrated that substance use is linked to friendship ties"
  - p. 14: "In non-AI samples, substance use homophily and receiver effects are consistently positive and often substantial in magnitude"
- **I2** Rees, Freng and Winfree: in heterogeneous schools, AI adolescents had fewer reciprocated friendships than White peers. The paper builds on this study and notes that it was descriptive. (R1(1), AR1)
  - p. 2: "finding that AI adolescents had fewer reciprocated friendships than White peers"
- **I3** Schaefer, Kornienko and Fox: depression homophily can arise from withdrawal by depressed adolescents, not from avoidance by others. The paper builds on this. (R2(3))
  - p. 4: "show that depression homophily can emerge from withdrawal by depressed egos rather than from avoidance by alters"
- **I4** A conventional assumption in the adolescent health literature: risky behavior is a primary determinant of youth relationships. The paper disputes it for this population. (R3(2))
  - p. 16: "that risky behavior is a primary determinant of relationships in youth, does not hold in our study"
- **I5** Adolescent network research: gender and grade homophily are among its most robust findings. The paper replicates them as a baseline. (R1(1))
  - p. 5: "gender and grade homophily are among the most robust findings in adolescent network research"
- **I6** Network interventions for substance use presume that friendships are partly founded on substance use. The paper disputes the presumption for AI youth. (R3(2))
  - p. 16: "often hinges on the presumption that friendship ties are, to some extent, founded on substance use behaviors"
