# Gold answers: Regional political climate's moderating role in the association between political conservatism and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in the United States

Paper `pone.0342063`, 12 pages. Status: drafted by the assistant, reviewed and corrected by the labeler on 2026-11-21.

Origin codes: R1 to R4 are reviewers with the review round in parentheses, AR1 and AR2 are the author responses, ED is the editor, and `paper` means the answer comes from the published paper alone.

## Question

Acceptable:

- **Q1** How regional political climate moderates the relationship between a person's political conservatism and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and uptake. (R3(1), R4(1), R4(2), R2(1))
  - p. 1: "The goal of the present research was to examine how political climate moderates the relationship between self-reported political conservatism and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and uptake"
  - p. 2: "we examined the moderating role of political climate on the association between individual political orientation and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy"
- **Q2** The research gap: personal political orientation and local political climate have each been studied, but little research had examined their interaction. (R2(1))
  - p. 2: "there has been little research on the interaction between personal political orientation and local political climate"

## Central claim

Acceptable:

- **C1** Political climate is tied to hesitancy and booster uptake mainly for conservatives: liberals are less hesitant wherever they live, while conservatives in liberal areas are less hesitant than conservatives in conservative areas. (R4(1), R4(2), R5(1))
  - p. 1: "conservatives living in liberal political climates were less vaccine hesitant than their conservative counterparts living in conservative regions"
  - p. 8: "has a stronger association with vaccine and booster hesitancy for individuals who are higher on political conservatism as compared to those who are politically liberal"
  - p. 10: "political climate in the US is associated with vaccine hesitancy and uptake for politically conservative individuals"

Rejected:

- **C-x1** Liberal political climates cause, or have an impact on, conservatives' willingness to vaccinate. (R4(2) quoting the revised draft's abstract, R2(1))
  - Reviewer 4 asked for causal wording to be removed. The published paper keeps only a hedged suggestion and states that no causal or directional reading is warranted.
  - p. 10: "this data is cross-sectional and we cannot assume causal or directional associations"

## Evidence

Acceptable:

- **E1** An online self-report survey of a nonprobability convenience sample, recruited in the United States through Qualtrics Panels between March 8 and April 19, 2023. (R2(1), R4(1), R4(2), ED(1))
  - p. 2: "This was an online self-reported study in which a nonprobability convenience sample was recruited from the United States between March 8 and April 19, 2023"
  - p. 3: "Participants were a convenience sample recruited through Qualtrics Panels"
- **E2** A final sample of 683 participants. Qualtrics collected data from 1,777 and marked 970 as failing quality screens; further exclusions followed for invalid or unmatched zip codes. (R2(1), R5(1), R3(1))
  - p. 5: "The final sample for these analyses included 683 participants"
  - p. 5: "Qualtrics Panels collected data from 1777 participants, and identified 970 participants as not passing all quality screens"
- **E3** Regional political climate is the percent Republican vote in the 2020 presidential election for the participant's zip code. (R2(1), R3(1), R4(1), R4(2), R5(1))
  - p. 3: "We used this % Republican vote by zip code as a measure of conservative regional climate"
- **E4** Political conservatism is the average of two self-rated items, social and economic, each on a 0 to 10 scale. (R2(1))
  - p. 3: "These two items were averaged to form a political conservatism scale"
- **E5** Hesitancy is measured with seven items adapted from the Attitudes towards Adult Vaccination Scale; vaccine and booster status are self-reported. (R2(1), R4(1))
  - p. 3: "was measured using the seven items from the Attitudes towards Adult Vaccination Scale"
  - p. 3: "Self-reported vaccine status and booster status were assessed through individual items"
- **E6** Linear regressions for hesitancy and logistic regressions for vaccine and booster status, each with a conservatism by climate interaction and demographic controls. (R4(1), ED(1))
  - p. 4: "Model 3 included all variables from Model 2 and the interaction between political conservatism and conservative regional climate"
  - p. 5: "we ran two sets of generalized linear models with logit link"
- **E7** The sample was recruited in four quota groups by race (Black or White only) and income (above or below the US median), because the data came from a larger project on marginalization. (R2(1), R5(1), ED(1), AR1)
  - p. 4: "the sample was recruited across four quota groups based on race and income"

## Key findings

Acceptable:

- **F1** [core] Political conservatism and a conservative regional climate are each associated with more vaccine and booster hesitancy and with lower vaccination and boosting. (R2(1), R4(2))
  - p. 10: "Political conservatism and conservative regional climate were both associated with higher levels of COVID-19 vaccine and booster hesitancy and lower rates of self-reported vaccination and boosting"
- **F2** [core] For hesitancy the two interact: liberals are low in hesitancy regardless of climate, and hesitancy rises with conservatism more steeply in conservative climates. (R4(1), R4(2), R5(1))
  - p. 10: "As political conservatism increases vaccine hesitancy also increases, but this increase is even greater for individuals who live in a more conservative regional climate"
  - p. 1: "such that liberals were less likely to be hesitant regardless of political climate"
- **F3** [core] For booster uptake the same interaction appears: conservatives in liberal climates are more likely to be boosted than conservatives in conservative climates (about 60% against under 35% for the most conservative), while liberals are likely to be boosted anywhere. (R4(1), R4(2), R5(1))
  - p. 8: "The most conservative participants in liberal climates were 60% likely to get boosted, while conservatives in conservative climates were less than 35% likely to get boosted"
  - p. 10: "conservative individuals are more likely to received booster doses in liberal regions"
- **F4** [secondary] There is no significant interaction for initial vaccination status; the authors attribute the difference to vaccine mandates. (paper)
  - p. 7: "The interaction between political conservatism and conservative regional climate (Table 6) was not significant"
  - p. 10: "likely reflects the high prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine mandates"
- **F5** [secondary] Vaccine hesitancy and booster hesitancy are strongly correlated (r = .84). (R2(1))
  - p. 5: "Vaccine hesitancy was positively correlated with booster hesitancy (r = .84"
- **F6** [secondary] Unvaccinated and unboosted participants were younger, had lower incomes, were more conservative and lived in more conservative climates. (R2(1))
  - p. 5: "Unvaccinated participants were significantly younger"
  - p. 5: "lived in more conservative regional climates"

Rejected:

- **F-x1** The study shows that social pressure, group polarization or social influence explains why conservatives in liberal areas are less hesitant. (R2(1) on the submitted draft, R4(2))
  - The draft asserted group polarization as the mechanism. The published paper names social pressure only as an example and says the mechanism was not assessed.
  - p. 10: "While we did not asses the mechanism through which this occurs"

## Scope and limits

Acceptable:

- **L1** Zip-code vote share is a proxy for political climate; other differences between zip codes, such as population density, could produce the associations. (R2(1), R4(1), R4(2), R5(1))
  - p. 10: "other zip code-based differences could be driving the associations noted in this paper"
- **L2** The data are cross-sectional, so no causal or directional conclusion follows. (R2(1), R4(1), R4(2))
  - p. 10: "this data is cross-sectional and we cannot assume causal or directional associations"
- **L3** Vaccine status is self-reported and open to social desirability and recall bias. (R2(1))
  - p. 10: "we cannot assume that reported vaccine status reflects actual vaccine status"
- **L4** The sample is an online panel of White and Black participants only and may not generalize to the wider US population. (ED(1), R5(1), R4(2), R2(1), AR1)
  - p. 10: "our sample includes only White and Black participants recruited through Qualtrics Panels, and may not generalize to broader populations in the United States"
- **L5** Effect modification by demographic factors was not tested, so the pooled associations are averages and Simpson's paradox cannot be excluded. (ED(2))
  - p. 10: "we cannot exclude the possibility that heterogeneity across demographic subgroups could produce aggregation effects such as Simpson’s paradox"
- **L6** The mechanism linking climate to conservatives' vaccination was not assessed. (R2(1), R4(1), R4(2))
  - p. 10: "While we did not asses the mechanism through which this occurs"

Rejected:

- **L-x1** The authors acknowledge that losing most of the initial sample to quality screens may bias the results. (R2(1), R5(1), R4(2))
  - Three reviewers raised attrition. The published paper reports the exclusions and never names them as a limitation, so this is a reviewer's criticism, not something the authors say.
- **L-x2** The hesitancy scale was modified without re-validation, which the authors note as a limitation. (R2(1), R4(1))
  - The authors answered this by adding a factor analysis and reliability figures; it is not a stated limitation.
  - p. 5: "The vaccine hesitancy items demonstrated adequate reliability"
- **L-x3** The authors note that no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. (R2(1))
  - Raised by a reviewer; the published paper does not mention it.

## Theoretical positioning

Acceptable:

- **not stated** is an acceptable answer for this slot.
- **T0** [primary] [framework] The paper names no theoretical framework. (R2(1))
- **T1** [secondary] [framework] The paper places itself in two empirical literatures: one linking individual political orientation to vaccine hesitancy, one linking regional political climate to it. (R2(1), R4(2))
  - p. 10: "Previous studies have focused on either political orientation or political climate"
- **T2** [stance] Combines and extends: it brings the two literatures together by testing their interaction, which it presents as a first. (R4(2), R1(1))
  - p. 7: "The present research provides the first assessment of the potential interaction between political orientation, political climate, and COVID vaccination"
- **T3** [secondary] [framework] The authors suggest social pressure as a possible explanation, without assessing it. (R4(1), R4(2))
  - p. 10: "these findings suggest that environmental factors (e.g., social pressure) may impact decisions to receive annual COVID-19 doses"

Rejected:

- **T-x1** The paper works within group polarization theory. (R2(1) quoting the submitted draft)
  - The draft's introduction invoked group polarization. Reviewer 2 called it name-dropping, and it does not appear in the published paper.
- **T-x2** The paper works within research on resistance to persuasion. (R2(1) quoting the submitted draft)
  - Also in the draft's introduction and absent from the published paper.
- **T-x3** The study is grounded in, or applies, social influence theory. (R4(1))
  - Reviewer 4 suggested these literatures. The published paper mentions social pressure once, as an example of a mechanism it did not assess. Flagged as an unsupported attribution (labeler, 2026-11-21).
  - p. 10: "these findings suggest that environmental factors (e.g., social pressure) may impact decisions to receive annual COVID-19 doses"

## Interlocutor claim

Acceptable:

- **I1** Prior US studies: political conservatism is consistently associated with greater COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and lower uptake. The paper builds on this and replicates it. (R2(1))
  - p. 2: "political conservatism has been consistently associated with greater COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy"
- **I2** Prior studies treat either political orientation or political climate, not both. The paper builds on them by examining the two together. (R2(1))
  - p. 2: "While previous research has identified the unique impact of both political orientation and political climate on vaccination attitudes"
  - p. 10: "Previous studies have focused on either political orientation or political climate"
- **I3** Prior area-level studies: Democratic vote share in 2020 is positively associated with vaccination and booster rates at county and state level. The paper builds on this with an individual-level test. (R4(1))
  - p. 2: "a positive association between Democratic vote share"
- **I4** Prior work: the partisan divide in hesitancy may be due in part to the politicization of COVID-19. The paper builds on this as background. (R2(1))
  - p. 2: "the increased partisan divide in vaccine hesitancy may be attributed, at least in part, to the politicization of COVID-19"
- **I5** Prior work: political orientation has become a better predictor of hesitancy than demographics, pandemic fear or trust in health institutions. The paper builds on this. (paper)
  - p. 2: "political orientation has become a better predictor of vaccine hesitancy than demographic variables, pandemic-related fear, and trust in health institutions"
- **I6** Prior research: people living in states with higher Republican vote shares were more likely to report vaccine hesitancy (ref. 21). The paper builds on this. Unlike I3, which ties a region's vote share to its aggregate vaccination rate, this ties it to individual respondents' reported hesitancy. (paper (added by the labeler, 2026-11-21))
  - p. 2: "those who lived in states with higher Republican vote shares in the 2020 presidential election were more likely to report vaccine hesitancy"

Rejected:

- **I-x1** The paper cites claims that President Trump downplayed the pandemic or called it a hoax. (R2(1) quoting the submitted draft)
  - In the draft, removed in revision. A model that gives this is not reading the published paper.
- **I-x2** The paper cites a study in which Republicans were more willing to vaccinate after watching a video of Trump. (R2(1) quoting the submitted draft)
  - In the draft, removed in revision.
