## 4. Online hostility · Scope and limits

**Gold answer:** Limited to American X/Twitter users; may not generalize to other platforms.

**The model's entries in this part (9):**

1. The main study concerns US users of a public, highly political platform in a deliberately selected polarized setting. It does not establish that the associations transfer to other countries, platforms, or private conversations.
2. The outcome is hostility grounded in political differences. It neither encompasses every toxic exchange nor singles out the narrower category of hate speech.
3. Even the validated text score can mistake vocabulary about political conflict for an expression of hostility. The authors interpret it as an aggregate indicator, not a definitive judgment about each post.
4. The observational results do not establish that engagement causes hostility. Within-person models account for stable differences but leave open time-varying confounding, measurement error, and influence in both directions.
5. The study does not establish the causal ordering of the engagement indicators or an effect of education on hostility independent of engagement.
6. The polarization measure concerns party leaders, not parties as institutions or their ordinary supporters; the study cannot say whether those other measures would yield the same conditional pattern.
7. The interaction estimates do not show that polarization itself turns engagement into hostility. Polarization could precede hostile interactions, accompany them, or follow them.
8. The study does not decide between grievance-based and personality-based explanations. In particular, adjustment for the Big Five does not directly test explanations based on sadism or Dark Triad traits, which the survey did not measure.
9. Posting and connection counts indicate potential reach only. Neither those counts nor the descriptive network graphs establish influence, centrality, or community structure.

## 21. Criminal record labels · Scope and limits

**Gold answer:** The design is transparent, so social desirability or demand effects may drive part of the results; the manipulation check eases this concern without ruling it out.

**The model's entries in this part (10):**

3. The effect of the mugshot image cannot be separated from the effect of the stigmatizing label.

## 29. Adolescent networks · Evidence

**Gold answer:** 263 students completed the survey (a 77% response rate); the analysis uses 247 with complete network data.

**The model's entries in this part (6):**

1. The data are cross-sectional friendship nomination networks of 9th- and 10th-grade students (N = 247) in three schools on a Northern Plains reservation, analyzed with exponential random graph models (ERGMs).
2. The three schools were chosen purposefully to span different settings: a small, isolated tribal school, a larger tribal school, and a private college-preparatory school. The overall response rate was 77%.
3. Students completed tablet-based surveys in Network Canvas, nominating up to 10 closest friends. Community and Youth Advisory Boards reviewed the measures.
4. Measures:
- Substance use: binary ever-use items.
- Depression: a six-item CES-D.
- AI identity: six items adapted from the MEIM Affirmation and Belonging subscale.
- Demographics: gender, grade and sexual orientation.
5. For each school, the authors fit a base ERGM plus separate models for each substance. These included structural terms (reciprocity, GWESP, degree terms, two-path) and sender, receiver and homophily terms. Average marginal effects were computed so effects could be compared across schools.
6. Missing self-reported substance use (refusals) was filled in from peer perceptions. Sensitivity analyses excluding these cases, along with substance-use-only models, served as robustness checks.

## 37. Adolescent networks · Scope and limits

**Gold answer:** Three schools on one reservation: the results may not extend to other reservations or to American Indian youth in racially heterogeneous schools.

**The model's entries in this part (9):**

1. The schools represent one reservation, not AI adolescents generally. The findings may not extend to racially mixed schools, and the authors could not obtain a suitable comparison sample of non-AI schools.
2. The survey captures one developmental period at one time point. It cannot show how friendships or behaviors change with age or through school transitions.
3. Cross-sectional ERGMs cannot separate choosing similar friends from becoming more similar to friends. The focal estimates therefore do not establish causation in either direction.
4. The demographic coefficients are descriptive rather than estimates of independent causal effects, because the adjustment set was designed for the focal predictors.
5. Peer reports used to fill in missing substance-use responses may overstate friends' use, and imputing from within the friendship network may inflate apparent similarity between friends. Excluding cases with imputed values changed the significance classification of a few estimates, mostly in School B.
6. The AI identity measure captures strength of affirmation, not necessarily cues peers can see. The school friendship networks also leave out much of the kinship and family life where identity may matter for relationships.
7. Differences among the three schools suggest contextual explanations but do not establish how community conditions or local tribal governance shape friendships.
8. The findings raise questions about the fit of existing prevention strategies but do not establish how to use the observed friendship patterns in an intervention.
9. The Tribe owns the data, and the research agreement restricts public access and prohibits naming the Tribe in publications.

## 38. COVID polarization · Scope and limits

**Gold answer:** The analyses are correlational and do not imply causation.

**The model's entries in this part (11):**

1. Semantic distance is a proxy, not a direct observation of disagreement. Averaging by user can obscure sarcasm, indirect disagreement, and other meanings not captured by the embeddings.
2. The Canadian activity classifier could not reliably distinguish parties on the same side. The analysis therefore does not resolve within-family differences or represent the Bloc Québécois and other excluded parties.
3. Party labels depend partly on what users report about themselves. Voter records are an imperfect check because registrations can be stale and names do not always match.
4. The findings describe a politically engaged subset of Twitter users, not the distribution of opinions in the U.S. or Canadian public.
5. English-only sampling leaves out much francophone discussion and makes Quebec’s high measured polarization especially difficult to interpret.
6. Location is inferred rather than verified. Inaccurate or fabricated profiles, VPNs, and automated accounts can undermine geographic attribution; bots may also enlarge the apparent partisan divide.
7. The national corpora were collected differently: U.S. keywords emphasized the election, while Canadian keywords more directly targeted COVID-19 and provinces. Some apparent country differences may reflect that sampling choice.
8. Canada’s small number of regions limits inference about both vote margins and vaccine uptake. The authors also identify Canadian vaccine mandates as a reason not to expect a straightforward comparison with the U.S.
9. Late-2020 tweets and later regional vaccination rates are not contemporaneous observations. Their correlation may have predictive value, but it does not establish that online division reduced uptake or isolate its effect from ideology, education, or other regional factors.
10. Matching peaks to news or policy announcements establishes timing, not causation. An external event could not be identified for every peak; online posts themselves are a possible explanation.
11. The study does not settle why conservative regions were often more polarized or quantify how much cross-border circulation and social networks themselves changed attitudes or behavior.

## 51. COVID polarization · Scope and limits

**Gold answer:** Party labels may be inaccurate, because the classification of users into partisan groups relies on self-reported information.

**The model's entries in this part (8):**
