Reviewer #1: This manuscript investigates the relationship between political engagement and political hostility on social media platforms, using a US-based twitter dataset + survey data.

The manuscript has an ambitious Scope, the study tackles an important and timely issue which is political hostility online and its "causes", using a combination of self-reported and behavioral data, which is rare to find in the literature.

The linkage of panel survey responses to large-scale Twitter data (over 9 million tweets) is commendable and logistically impressive. The use of Random-Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models demonstrates an effort to address concerns of endogeneity and individual heterogeneity. Also, the authors attempt to validate their findings using multiple measures of hostility (word embeddings, sentiment dictionaries, Perspective API) is valuable for robustness of results and generalizability across different proxies of political hostility. Regression analysis is performed with statistical rigour. In general, the manuscript contributes to a growing body of work on the “dark side” of political engagement and affective polarization.

I have, however, some concerns with conceptual vagueness and terminological issues. The term "precursors" appears throughout the manuscript without being explicitly defined. Is it used interchangeably with antecedents or predictors? Its ambiguous use weakens clarity. Similarly, the shift between “political hate,” “political hostility,” and “toxicity” is insufficiently delineated. If the aim is to measure political hostility more broadly than hate speech, the embedding approach centered on a “political hate” vector appears conceptually inconsistent. There is no theoretical or empirical justification for using this specific vector (i.e., “political” + “hate”) as a proxy for political hostility. Affective polarization is treated as a moderator, but the measure is not described in sufficient detail. How is it constructed? Why is it not included in the main text?

Moreover, I have concerns about the methodology. The description of the Super-Unsupervised (SU) embedding-based hostility measure is unclear. It is not clear how the “political hate” vector is constructed, it is not clear how the overall similarity of the tweets to the political hate vector is obtained (summed? averaged? something else?). Moreover, is it a good proxy? How is this methodology based than sentiment analysis or topic modeling or other ML-based methods to label political hostility? The, several methods (e.g., count models, time-series cross-section with pooled OLS, network centrality metrics, and others) are mentioned without explanation or rationale, assuming prior knowledge that is not standard for the generalist audience of PLOS ONE. Finally, the analysis pipeline lacks structure and coherence. The methodological section is disorganized, with unnecessary technicalities (e.g., software packages used for plotting) and insufficient detail on actual statistical models. Moreover, data availability is not confirmed at review time, violating PLOS ONE’s policy, if I interpreted it correctly.

Since my expertise is more on network science than in text mining or inference analysis, I am more confident in saying that the section on online network structure and echo chambers is conceptually underdeveloped and technically weak. I would suggest to either remove it or improve it. Terms like “centrality,” “echo chamber,” and “influence” are used without proper definitions or references and there is a lack of consideration of a vast body of literature on these matters. Stating that some nodes have a lot of connections is not the proper way to assess that these are not in an echo chamber. Visualizations (e.g., Figure 3) are superficial and not analytically informative. No clear metrics (e.g., modularity, assortativity, degree centrality distributions) are presented, which would instead be more informative than the presented figures. The authors may benefit from involving a network science expert to refine both the methodological design and interpretation. The idea of considering the influence of the social network on political hostility is a good idea in my opinion, but the current realization is not sufficiently sound.

I also believe that there is a missed chance to perform a longitudinal network analysis on the evolution of political hostility in relation to partisan interactions: is hostility fuelled by cross-partisan interactions? Are these central users more hostile because they interact with a wider variety of political stances?

There are also some minor issues I would like to mention. The manuscript contains numerous grammatical errors and awkward phrasing, including in the abstract (“fuels hostile” should be “fuels hostility,” “it is political engagement that are driving” → “is driving”). Overly informal language is used in the introduction (e.g., “as any regular user of social media will know”), which is not appropriate for a scientific manuscript. The writing could benefit from editing for conciseness. Redundancy is common, particularly in the background sections. The structure of the manuscript is unclear and deviates from conventional scientific organization. The division of sections is idiosyncratic, and critical methods and results are often buried in appendices. Most relevantly, important results and definitions should not be relegated to the appendices. The main text lacks depth in empirical description.

An additional doubt is: if the authors are affiliated to a Danish university, why performing a US based study? The literature is full of US based analyses especially on the topic of polarization and harmful phenomena on online social media, why not shifting the analysis on a different population?

Recommendation: Major Revision

If the authors are to revise, they should:

1. Clearly define all conceptual constructs, measures, analyses, and models, addressing the concerns stressed above. Justify the use of the word vector "political hate" or change the proxy used to estimate tweets political hostility level.

2. Reorganize the manuscript to follow a standard scientific structure (Introduction, Related Work, Data and Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion), or at least improve writing style and structure.

3. Include methodological details and key robustness results in the main text, not just in appendices.

4. Improve the network analysis or remove it and enhance the rest of the contributions

Reviewer #2: This paper links survey measures of political engagement and resources to behavioral indicators of online political hostility among U.S. Twitter/X users. It finds that (i) engagement is positively associated with hostile political content; (ii) this association is stronger among individuals high in affective polarization; and (iii) hostile users have more friends/followers.

Overall: The paper's questions are important, its dataset is impressive, and the findings are useful.

That said, there are major methodological concerns - especially the construct validity of the hostility measure, underspecified network methods, and overly causal language.

### Major issues ###

## Measurement

My main concern is how political hostility is measured.

The main approach uses word2vec embeddings trained on panelists’ tweets and distances to a “political hate” vector (“political” + “hate”). To me, this seems like a highly questionable method. Centrally, it risks capturing whether a user talks about “hate” and “politics”, rather than the _hostile properties_ of the message.

The reported “74 accuracy” against expert labels does not increase confidence in the measure. Accuracy should not be used as a measure, and is in particular not informative without class balance, thresholding, and error decomposition. It is often misleading for imbalanced classes. Please report: baseline accuracy, precision/recall/F1, ROC/AUC, confusion matrix, and whether validation is in-domain or on a held-out set. Also report correlations with AFINN, Perspective API, and the supervised classifier mentioned in the appendix; bring key validation summaries into the main text.

The lexicon-based sentiment measure is not convincing for two reasons: (1) sentiment is conceptually misaligned with hostility; (2) lexicon methods often perform poorly on social media language.

To me, the only measure with some face validity here is Perspective API toxicity - imperfect, but widely used and at least more or less aimed at the target construct.

The problem with just measuring toxicity (rather than _political toxicity_) is that the observed link between “engagement” and “toxicity” in theory might be driven by e.g., sports trash talk. If the research question is _political hostility_, you do need to restrict toxicity scoring to tweets classified as political or interact toxicity with a political-tweet indicator.

If feasible, consider training a modern supervised model (e.g., fine-tuned BERT or a carefully set up zero-shot LLM) and validate it with established protocols and best practices. In all cases, provide full detail on preprocessing, model choices, and validation. And use correct metric for the validation.

On the other hand, because the SU score averages across each user’s tweets, heavy political tweeters may be mechanically closer to “political hate.” So you need to control for share of political tweets (not just volume) and replicate results on matched subsets (e.g., one random political tweet per user per week). The split of “political” vs “hostility” components that's in the appendix is potentially useful here. Maybe move a concise version to the main text to show the political component is not driving it.

## Network analysis

The network analysis also fails to convince. The claim that hostile users “dominate the network” and are not confined to echo chambers is insufficiently substantiated. The manuscript says very little about the network used. How was the network actually made (follower, retweet, mention? snapshot timing? sample boundaries?) What are the risks for interpretation if the graph includes only users in the analytic dataset? There seems to be important issues here.

Moreover, you need to support these claims with proper network measures, not just the rather hard-to-interpret visual that is Figure 3. For instance, assortativity/mixing by hostility, E/I index or modularity for partitions, and comparisons of centrality (degree, eigenvector, betweenness) with uncertainty (e.g., bootstrap or permutation tests). These key numerical evidence should be in the main text, not only in figures. As it stands, Figure 3 is difficult to interpret and may not warrant inclusion without clearer methods and metrics.

## Overstated causality

The manuscript uses causal verbs (“fuels,” “leads to,” “dominates”) that are not supported by the identification strategy. Please tone down causal claims throughout and frame results as associations. Acknowledge alternative mechanisms (topic exposure, elite cues, event-driven spikes) and clarify the limits of the models you employ.

## Ethics and data-sharing

I find the claim that this does not require IRB under Danish law highly surprising. This does not seem compatible with GDPR? I'm moreover not sure if either this nor the data availability statement is compatible with the requirements of the journal, but I leave these questions for the editor.

### Minor issues ###

- Fix text artifacts likely from PDF export (e.g., “a!ective,” “e!cacy”), typos, and double-parenthesis in citaitons

- Is it 'Twitter' or 'X'? I don't really care, but you should stick to one.

- Make sure all references are complete and consistently formatted.

In sum, the study tackles an important problem with valuable data. But there are potentially serious concerns with core aspects of its methodology. I hope to be convinced of the measures' validity by a revised version.

4. Reviewer #2

1. Measurement validity (main concern)

Comment: The embedding approach may capture "talking about hate + politics" rather than hostile properties; 74% accuracy is not informative.

Response:

We agree that an embedding approach could in principle pick up meta-talk about ‘hate’. This is why, we in the BJPS paper on the super-unsupervised method, inspected the nearest neighbours of the ‘political’, ‘hate’, and combined ‘political hate’ vectors. In our data, the neighbours are terms such as ‘hateful’, ‘racist’, ‘intolerance’, ‘supremacist’, and ‘partisan’, that is, terms that are both political and clearly hostile, indicating that the model is capturing hostility-in-use rather than mere mentions of the word ‘hate’. We now report this inspection in the main text.

We accept that accuracy alone is insufficient. In the original validation of the super-unsupervised (SU) measure we used 2,469 expert-coded tweets and obtained 74% agreement against a 50% baseline because the expert labels were balanced. We have now added (i) the baseline accuracy, (ii) precision, recall, and F1, and (iii) the confusion matrix for the held-out test set. We also clarify that the metrics are based on a train/test split of the expert-labelled tweets, which addresses the in-domain/held-out concern.

1. Theoretical framing of political engagement and its antecedents

Comment: The theorization was muddy. The manuscript treated education, internal efficacy, interest, knowledge, and participation as a grab bag of “political resources.” It was unclear why internal efficacy or participation should cause hostility, and participation and hostility may both be caused by political sophistication.

Response: We agree that the previous version treated conceptually distinct variables too similarly. We have revised the theoretical section to distinguish among three categories. Education and internal efficacy are now described as distal antecedents that may equip citizens with skills, confidence, and perceived competence in politics. Political interest and political knowledge are described as motivational and cognitive indicators of engagement. Online and offline participation are described as participatory engagement, that is, behavioral expressions of engagement. We also clarified that we do not claim that internal efficacy or participation necessarily has a direct causal effect on hostility. Rather, our claim is that politically engaged citizens, and citizens with antecedents that facilitate engagement, may be more likely to enter political conflict on X/Twitter, including antagonistic exchanges. The revised manuscript explicitly discusses the alternative possibility that political sophistication or partisan involvement is a common cause of both participation and hostility. We therefore formulate the expectations in associational terms and avoid treating all indicators as a single undifferentiated resource construct. The supplementary factor analysis is also used to support the decision to treat the indicators separately rather than as one scale.

2. Logic and causal status of affective-polarization moderation

Comment: The moderation analysis using affective polarization was theoretically unclear and not causally identified. Alternative causal models needed to be discussed.

Response: We agree. We added a dedicated theoretical subsection, “Affective polarization as a conditional context.” In the revised manuscript, the logic is that engagement provides attention, motivation, skills, and opportunities to participate in political discussion, while affective polarization may shape whether such participation is expressed in antagonistic terms toward political opponents. We also now explicitly discuss alternative causal models. Affective polarization may increase political interest or participation; political sophistication may increase both affective polarization and hostility; or hostile online interactions may increase affective polarization over time. Because the moderation analyses are observational, we now interpret them as descriptive evidence about conditional associations rather than as causally identified moderation effects.

3. Strength of claims about affective polarization

Comment: The claims about affective polarization were too strong because only some interaction terms were statistically significant.

Response: We agree and have revised the wording throughout the manuscript. The abstract, results, and discussion now state that the moderation evidence is partial and uneven across indicators. These analyses reinforce the cautious interpretation: the moderation results are most consistent for participatory engagement and should be treated as exploratory for the remaining indicators. We no longer describe the affective-polarization expectations as uniformly confirmed.
