Reviewer #1: Review PLOS ONE: PONE-D-25-35736

This paper investigates the structure of friendship networks among adolescents of native American descent in three schools (263 students in total). The study aims to examine whether network processes characteristic of students without native American descent are different compared to those surveyed in their study. To this end, the authors employ statistical network models and estimate one exponential random graph model for each school. While the goals of the manuscript are intriguing, I have several concerns regarding the theoretical framework and the analysis. I hope my comments are helpful and can serve the authors in further developing their manuscript.

Mismatch between claimed contribution and conducted analyses.

My biggest issue with the manuscript is that the authors claim to study whether network processes differ for Native American students compared to non-Native American students. Yet, all students in their survey are of native American descent; consequently, they can only make rather crude qualitative comparisons with past work, and the generalizability of their findings is very limited. Past work indicated that tie-formation mechanisms differ between contexts according to various characteristics, such as size (McFarland et al. 2014), and that processes connected to racial and ethnic intergroup relations are linked to schools’ demographic composition (Kruse and Kroneberg 2019; Moody 2001; Smith et al. 2016; Zhao 2023).

Hence, drawing overall conclusions regarding tie-formation mechanisms among AI-youth might be difficult without a sample of non-AI-youth students. A potential solution for this issue would be to search for schools in the Add Health dataset or another US-based dataset containing friendship networks among students that are similar in terms of size, racial homogeneity, geographical location (rural area), etc. Ideally, the comparison dataset would be obtained during the last few years to avoid potential time trends in network formation mechanisms. Thereby, the authors could run the same model specifications in these schools and show whether tie-formation mechanisms differ between AI and non-AI schools in a more convincing way.

Currently, the findings overall are showing very similar patterns as other samples (e.g., transitivity, gender homophily, and classroom homophily are present in almost all school friendship networks), and it's not entirely clear to me what the novel contribution of the paper is. The finding that substance use is not predictive of students’ friendship choices, unlike reported in other studies, is interesting, but I don’t think that the authors can conclude from the current analysis that this finding stems from different tie-formation mechanisms among AI-youth. Prior work shows that there is variation in the extent to which substance use structures friendship networks (Gommans et al. 2016; Pearson et al. 2006; Wittek and Xu 2025), and that the authors do not find statistically significant effects could be due to other characteristics of the schools in the sample (e.g., all are located in a rural area, rather small in size, racially homogeneous, etc.). Again, a comparison with similar non-AI schools could help in this regard and enhance the generalizability of the findings. I noticed that there is a study looking at the position of AI-youth in the school networks in the Add Health data that might be highly relevant for the authors: (Rees, Freng, and Winfree, 2014).

Theoretical framework

Another major issue that I see with the manuscript is that the theoretical foundation of the analysis could be much stronger. In particular, it did not become sufficiently clear to me why we should expect that there are differences in tie-formation mechanisms between AI and non-AI youth. Currently, the theory section feels more like a literature review for an explorative analysis, and there are few arguments of why we would expect different network processes for AI-youth. I think the arguments regarding the importance of kinship and community in AI-youth currently mentioned are a good starting point, but they have to be elaborated more and it could be beneficial if the authors derive hypotheses related to specific network processes. For instance, if AI-youth have a strong sense of community and many out-of-school activities in their villages/ a vibrant social life carried by local ties and tradition, wouldn’t we expect that network processes pointing to cohesion, such as transitivity and reciprocity, are stronger in these schools than in similar schools visited by non-AI youth? Qualitative studies on AI-youth (e.g., Peshkin 2013) could be an addition that strengthens the theoretical reasoning as these ethnographies might have descriptions of AI-youth's social life that help the authors to form hypotheses about differences with non-AI youth (see, e.g., Milner 2013).

Another theoretical angle that I felt was underexplored by the authors is the role of AI identity for network formation. There is a range of studies concerned with ethnic identity and friendship choices in European classrooms that highlight the importance of ethnic identity for friendship networks (Leszczensky et al. 2016; Leszczensky and Pink 2019), and these studies might provide some ideas for hypotheses related to AI-identity.

Methods

The ERGMs are solidly executed and the GOF is OK. However, I had a hard time reading the results from the presented plots and would suggest larger plots. Some coefficients are small due to the scaling of specific variables, and it could be a good idea to adjust the axis for these coefficients accordingly. Also, since ERGMs have a non-linear link function, it is non-trivial to compare estimates between contexts, and I would suggest using average marginal effects for this purpose (Duxbury 2019, 2021b, 2021a). Average marginal effects would also allow for a more nuanced comparison of the strength of network effects found in the authors’ dataset with a comparison sample of non-AI youth. Currently, the analysis can only identify whether there is a statistically significant effect or not, but there might be more subtle variations, e.g., a 10% lower Average marginal effect for reciprocity in non-AI youth compared to AI-youth, that are missed by the current analytical strategy. Also, it would be good to report models only including the variables for substance use so that readers can see whether the lack of substance-use-related effects is due to multicollinearity with other effects in the models (for a discussion of the issue of interpreting ERGM coefficients in complex model specifications see, e.g., Martin 2020).

Summary

While I think the efforts of the authors in obtaining this unique dataset and applying network models to it are commendable, I see much room for further improvement of the manuscript. From my perspective, a key adjustment would be to include a comparison sample of non-AI schools with similar properties, estimate average marginal effects in the post estimation to have a stronger generalizability of the findings, and a methodologically more rigorous analytical strategy. Additionally, the theory section could be improved by including more arguments and references that are specific to AI-youths’ social life. Why would we expect different network processes for this population, and how exactly should tie-formation mechanisms differ for AI youth? These questions could be answered more convincingly by the theoretical framework and might also pave the way for further analysis, revealing interesting patterns. I wish the authors good luck with the manuscript and hope that some of my comments are helpful for the research process.

Reviewer #2: General Assessment:

This manuscript represents a valuable contribution to the understudied area of adolescent social networks within American Indian (AI) communities. The study is grounded in a promising and contextually rich dataset, and its focus holds the potential to advance our understanding of network dynamics in culturally specific settings. That said, the manuscript currently lacks argumentative depth in some sections, and additional steps are needed to ensure the robustness and clarity of the reported findings. The comments below are intended to support the authors in strengthening both the conceptual framework and methodological rigor of their work.

Comments:

Formatting:

In-text citations are consistently placed after the punctuation, rather than before it. This should be corrected to align with standard citation formatting practices.

Introduction (Lines 113–146):

While the discussion of endogenous network processes is technically accurate, it reads more like a textbook overview than a targeted theoretical framing. The authors should clearly articulate why these processes are especially relevant or potentially less so in the context of AI communities. Drawing on research about social norms or relational expectations in AI cultural contexts could enrich the interpretation and provide a more tailored analytical framework.

In Lines 114–116, the claim that structural processes occur "independently of individual characteristics or other exogenous factors" omits the role of social norms in shaping such processes (e.g., reciprocity as a norm-driven behaviour, “if someone is kind to me, I am kind in return”). This should be acknowledged and theoretically integrated.

Methods:

The manuscript does not address how missing data were handled. A discussion of the proportion, pattern, and treatment of missing data is essential to assess the validity of the analyses.

In the section “Endogenous Factors” (Lines 245–267), a notable redundancy arises in the description of the structural network parameters. This duplication stems from the earlier section of the introduction (Lines 113–146), where these parameters were already discussed in detail but without a clear connection to the specific research questions..

Measures:

The scale for measuring American Indian identity (Lines 282–289) would benefit from an assessment of psychometric quality, such as internal consistency measures (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) as it does not seem to be a validated scale.

Results:

Figure 1 is informative. However, to improve interpretability and facilitate use in future meta-analyses, I recommend including supplementary tables that display the full model outputs separately for each school.

Lines 378–408 discuss results in comparison with prior literature during the results section. Such interpretive commentary should be reserved for the discussion section to avoid the impression that the models also provide findings about non-AI populations.

To address potential overcontrol bias in the models, especially given the non-significant findings related to substance use, depression, and AI identity, I suggest two robustness checks:

First, provide a correlation matrix of the core constructs, including these three variables.

Second, if high intercorrelations are observed, consider estimating separate ERGMs for each construct within each school to reduce the risk of suppressor effects or multicollinearity distorting the substantive conclusions.

Discussion:

Lines 411–448 contain references to "previous research" without clearly citing specific studies. Given the detailed referencing in the results section, the paper would benefit from explicitly citing sources here.

The limitations section (Lines 484–480) is relatively brief and lacks a substantive discussion of the implications of the stated limitations. Embedding these limitations within the broader research context would offer a more transparent and critical reflection on the study’s constraints.

Abstract

First, the abstract should be revised slightly to avoid giving the impression that the paper primarily examines peer influence on substance use. In lines 5–7, the formulation “American Indian (AI) adolescents experience disproportionately high rates of substance use, yet the peer network mechanisms that may influence these behaviors within AI-majority reservation schools remain understudied” could be read as suggesting that the study investigates how peer networks influence substance use. However, the analyses focus on friendship formation and, more specifically, on whether substance use is associated with peer selection, sending ties, receiving ties, and homophily. I recommend rephrasing this part so that the abstract more clearly reflects the actual analytical focus: the role of substance use and other characteristics in friendship formation.

Introduction

Second, on page 4, line 7, the abbreviation “AI” appears in the main text without being introduced there. Even though the abbreviation is defined elsewhere, the first use in the main text should introduce the term as “American Indian (AI)” before using the abbreviation throughout the manuscript.

Methods

Second, the treatment of demographic variables should be clarified. On page 10, the authors write: “We include gender, grade, and sexual orientation as control variables.” Because these variables are introduced in the theoretical section, the reader may expect them to be part of the substantive argument rather than merely adjustment variables. The authors may find it useful to consider the logic discussed by Westreich and Greenland (2013) regarding the “Table 2 fallacy”: coefficients for adjustment variables should not automatically be interpreted as independent substantive effects unless the model was specified for that purpose. In the present manuscript, this distinction matters because some variables are focal predictors, whereas others appear to serve mainly as controls for demographic sorting.

In the present case, the imputed attribute is derived from peers’ perceptions of the respondent. Because these peers are themselves embedded in the friendship network, such imputations could in principle introduce or amplify behavioral similarity among connected adolescents if peer perceptions are shaped by the reporter’s own behavior or by friendship-group norms. I do not think this invalidates the approach, especially since the authors report sensitivity analyses excluding these cases.
